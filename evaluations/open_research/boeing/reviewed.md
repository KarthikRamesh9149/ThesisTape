# Investment research memo

As of 2024-08-01

**Research question**
As of 1 August 2024, what did Boeing agree to pay for Spirit AeroSystems, how was the consideration structured, and what could prevent the acquisition from closing or delivering its intended benefits?

Some parts of the question could not be established from eligible evidence.

## Answer

As of 2024-08-01, Boeing had agreed to acquire Spirit AeroSystems but had not closed the deal. Spirit shareholders were to receive Boeing common stock with an announced equity value of approximately $4.7 billion, based on a reference price of $37.25 per Spirit share. The larger $8.3 billion figure was the approximate total transaction value including Spirit’s last reported net debt—not the amount payable to shareholders. [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20240721010130/https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2024-07-01-Boeing-to-Acquire-Spirit-AeroSystems) [2](https://web.archive.org/web/20240714161646/https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/spirit-aerosystems-announces-acquisition-by-boeing-in-8-3-billion-transaction-302186491.html)

## Evidence

The exchange ratio targeted $37.25 per Spirit share, not a guaranteed dollar payout. Between the price limits, the number of Boeing shares per Spirit share equaled $37.25 divided by Boeing’s volume-weighted average share price over the 15-trading-day period ending on the second trading day before closing. At or below $149.00, the ratio was fixed at 0.25; at or above $206.94, it was fixed at 0.18. Outside those limits, the share count stopped adjusting, so a lower Boeing price could reduce the value received or a higher price could increase it. The final ratio and market value were not yet knowable. [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20240721010130/https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2024-07-01-Boeing-to-Acquire-Spirit-AeroSystems)

## Outlook

The companies expected the merger to close in mid-2025, but it could be delayed or fall through if Spirit did not complete the sale of specified Airbus-related operations, Spirit shareholders did not approve the merger, regulators withheld approval, or other closing conditions went unmet. Spirit and Airbus had a binding term sheet; signing definitive agreements would mark progress, but only completing the sale would satisfy the divestiture condition. Separately, certain events or changes could give Boeing, Spirit, or both the right to terminate the merger agreement. [2](https://web.archive.org/web/20240714161646/https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/spirit-aerosystems-announces-acquisition-by-boeing-in-8-3-billion-transaction-302186491.html) [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20240721010130/https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2024-07-01-Boeing-to-Acquire-Spirit-AeroSystems) [3](https://web.archive.org/web/20240721001822/https://boeing.mediaroom.com/news-releases-statements?item=131443)

Even if the merger closed on schedule, integrating Spirit into Boeing could prove harder or costlier than expected, and the anticipated synergies and quality improvements could arrive late or not at all. Sustained production-quality gains and evidence that operating savings outweigh integration costs would support the case for the deal. The closing itself would establish neither, and the disclosures did not establish how large or how soon any gains would be. [3](https://web.archive.org/web/20240721001822/https://boeing.mediaroom.com/news-releases-statements?item=131443) [2](https://web.archive.org/web/20240714161646/https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/spirit-aerosystems-announces-acquisition-by-boeing-in-8-3-billion-transaction-302186491.html)
