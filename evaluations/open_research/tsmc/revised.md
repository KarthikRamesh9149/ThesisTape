# Investment research memo

As of 2024-04-10

**Research question**
As of 10 April 2024, what financing had the U.S. Department of Commerce proposed for TSMC Arizona, how did TSMC’s planned investment and third fab fit the proposal, and which conditions could change what TSMC actually received?

Some parts of the question could not be established from eligible evidence.

## Answer

As of 2024-04-10, the U.S. Department of Commerce had proposed up to $6.6 billion in direct CHIPS Act funding for TSMC Arizona and, separately, up to $5 billion in repayable loans. The proposal would support TSMC’s expansion from a $40 billion plan for two Arizona fabs to more than $65 billion for three new leading-edge fabs in Phoenix—an increase of more than $25 billion in planned company investment, not federal funding. The nonbinding preliminary memorandum was neither a final award nor evidence of payment or borrowing. [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20240410192324/https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/04/biden-harris-administration-announces-preliminary-terms-tsmc-expanded) [2](https://web.archive.org/web/20240408131301/https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/08/us-to-award-tsmc-6-6b-in-grants-5b-in-loans-to-step-up-chip-manufacturing-in-arizona/) [3](https://web.archive.org/web/20240408195043/https://www.azfamily.com/2024/01/19/tsmc-says-its-40-billion-chip-project-phoenix-faces-further-delay/)

TSMC planned to add the third fab to the two already under construction and manufacture 2-nanometer or more advanced chips there. Its commitment was to build the fab before the end of the decade; that was not a confirmed date for volume production. [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20240410192324/https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/04/biden-harris-administration-announces-preliminary-terms-tsmc-expanded) [2](https://web.archive.org/web/20240408131301/https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/08/us-to-award-tsmc-6-6b-in-grants-5b-in-loans-to-step-up-chip-manufacturing-in-arizona/)

The separate 25 percent investment tax credit applied to qualifying investment. It was neither a Commerce grant nor a loan. The announced project cost does not establish TSMC’s eligible tax basis, so it cannot establish the credit TSMC would claim. [4](https://web.archive.org/web/20240111121004/https://www.warren.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/2024.01.09%20Letter%20to%20Commerce%20re.%20CHIPS%20revolving%20door.pdf) [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20240410192324/https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/04/biden-harris-administration-announces-preliminary-terms-tsmc-expanded)

## Outlook

The base case was a risk to payment timing rather than a cut in the proposed amount. The reported second-fab delay to 2027 or 2028 was a project risk, not an announced funding condition. If a final agreement tied a payment to a production milestone, a delay could postpone that payment; if the milestone accommodated the revised schedule, the earlier delay alone would not imply a loss. A binding award below the proposed ceiling would instead establish a reduction in funding. The first evidence to watch was the signed amount and payment conditions, followed by TSMC’s performance against any agreed milestones. [5](https://web.archive.org/web/20240405114651/https://www.manufacturingdive.com/news/tsmc-delays-second-arizona-chip-factory-to-2027/704937/) [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20240410192324/https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/04/biden-harris-administration-announces-preliminary-terms-tsmc-expanded)
