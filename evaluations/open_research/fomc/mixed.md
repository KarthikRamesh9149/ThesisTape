# Investment research memo

As of 2023-03-22

**Research question**
As of 22 March 2023, how did the Federal Reserve change its federal funds target range, and how did banking stress affect its policy guidance and the outlook for credit?

Some parts of the question could not be established from eligible evidence.

## Answer

On 2023-03-22, the Federal Open Market Committee raised the federal funds target range to 4-3/4 to 5 percent despite banking stress. The March statement confirms the new range but does not give the previous range or the size of the increase. Reuters reported a quarter of a percentage point as a pre-decision expectation, not as confirmation of the actual move. [2](https://web.archive.org/web/20230322080608/https://www.reuters.com/markets/rates-bonds/feds-powell-faces-political-storm-policy-minefield-over-svb-oversight-2023-03-22/#:~:text=WASHINGTON%2C%20March%2022%20%28Reuters%29%20-%20The%20Federal%20Reserve%20is,furor%20over%20Fed%20officials%27%20stock%20trading%20erupted%20in%202021.)

## Evidence

Bank distress was observable; an economy-wide credit contraction was not established. Silicon Valley Bank had failed on March 10. The Fed expected the turmoil to make credit harder to obtain for households and businesses and thereby weigh on activity, hiring and inflation. It did not report how far lenders had already pulled back. Powell’s reported comparison to a quarter-point hike described a possible economic effect, not a measured amount of lost credit or a hike the Fed could safely skip. The March statement said “some additional policy firming may be appropriate,” leaving further hikes possible without committing to their timing or size. Powell explained why banking stress mattered for that guidance: if banks materially restricted lending, monetary policy could have less work to do. That was a reason to keep the rate path conditional, not a signal that the Fed was done raising rates. The earlier FOMC statement is not in the available record, so the exact change in wording cannot be verified from these passages. [1](https://web.archive.org/web/20230322180056/https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20230322a.htm#:~:text=The%20U.S.%20banking%20system%20is%20sound%20and%20resilient.%20Recent,pressures%20and%20inflation%20expectations%2C%20and%20financial%20and%20international%20developments.) [4](https://web.archive.org/web/20230322233150/https://www.axios.com/2023/03/22/fed-raises-rates-silicon-valley-bank#:~:text=It%27s%20also%20possible%20that%20the%20bank%20stresses%20may%20affect,that%20monetary%20policy%20%22may%20have%20less%20work%20to%20do.%22)

## Outlook

The base case is that banks become more selective, reducing the need for additional hikes relative to a path without banking stress. Broad tightening of lending standards would support that view more clearly than falling loan volumes alone, since borrowers may also be seeking less credit. The strongest alternative is that the stress stays contained: stable lending standards alongside persistent inflation would weaken the case for relying on banks to supply that restraint.
